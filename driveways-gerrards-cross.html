A town built for big frontages

Gerrards Cross barely existed before the railway opened in 1906; within a generation it had become one of the most prosperous commuter settlements in England, and its housing stock still shows it. The roads fanning out from the station and around the Common — Camp Road, the Dukes Wood and Latchmoor areas, the avenues off Packhorse Road — carry substantial Edwardian and interwar villas set well back on deep plots, joined over the decades by large modern replacement houses that kept the generous setbacks.

That geometry defines the work. A typical Gerrards Cross frontage runs 80–150 m², often with an in-and-out carriage sweep, a parking court in front of a double garage, and mature trees the design has to respect. Projects here are as much groundworks and drainage as surfacing: the ground across much of the town is slow-draining clay, and a big paved catchment on clay needs its soakaways sized honestly — the difference between a driveway that shrugs off a storm and one that ponds at the garage door every November.

Conservation notes for Gerrards Cross

Gerrards Cross wears its protections more lightly than the old coaching towns — most of its housing is Edwardian or later — but they're real: the Common and its fringes anchor a designated conservation area, parts of the surrounding district carry Article 4 directions, and the whole town sits against the Chilterns National Landscape's design expectations. For driveways the practical points are boundary works and scale: gates, walls and enclosures facing the Common attract attention, and very large impermeable areas are exactly what the over-5 m² permeable-surfacing rules were written for. Permeable surfaces — resin bound, gravel, permeable block — keep even a 150 m² frontage inside permitted development; the rules are unpacked in our planning permission guide and conservation areas guide.

South along the A413, Chalfont St Peter's village core brings the more classic conservation-area considerations; we cover the Chalfonts from the same patch, so the designation check is the same first step on every visit.

Between the towns

Our Gerrards Cross round naturally takes in the surrounding lanes and villages: Chalfont St Peter immediately north, Chalfont St Giles beyond it up the Misbourne valley, and the plots out toward Fulmer and Denham to the south. The bigger the plot, the more the hybrid approach pays: gravel for the long approach, a bound surface where cars turn and park, setts at the entrance. It's the configuration we price most often here — and the one that keeps a six-figure-feeling frontage inside a five-figure budget.

Gerrards Cross questions we hear most

What's the best surface for an in-and-out carriage drive?

The hybrid in our worked example: stabilised gravel on the sweeps, where cars roll straight and the area is biggest, with a bound surface — resin or setts — on the parking court where they turn hard. All-resin looks superb and costs roughly a third more; all-gravel churns at the turning points. The zoned design gives you each surface where it's strongest.

Why does my existing driveway pond every winter?

Almost certainly the clay. Much of Gerrards Cross sits on slow-draining ground, and older driveways were built with little or no dedicated drainage — the water simply has nowhere to go once the ground saturates in November. The fix is usually a correctly sized crate soakaway and falls that actually reach it; sometimes it's as simple as a silted channel drain. We diagnose before we quote.

Can a 150 m² frontage really avoid planning permission?

Yes — the permeable-surfacing rules have no upper size limit. Resin bound, gravel and permeable block are permitted development at any area, provided the build-up genuinely drains. The caveats are boundary works facing the Common and any street-specific Article 4 direction, both checked on the design visit.
