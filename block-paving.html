The right block for a period frontage

Walk the older streets of Beaconsfield Old Town or the Chalfonts and the driveways that look right have something in common: muted, natural tones and simple, traditional patterns. The ones that jar are the bright multi-coloured paviours and stamped-concrete imitations — the exact surfaces the Chilterns Buildings Design Guide warns against as suburbanising.

Clay pavers are our first recommendation for period properties. Clay holds its colour permanently (the colour is the material, not a pigment), weathers to a soft patina rather than fading, and its slightly irregular edges suit older elevations. It costs more per block than concrete but usually outlasts the house's next two owners.

Setts — granite, sandstone or tumbled concrete formats — bring texture to entrances, aprons and borders. A sett apron at the road edge with a quieter field of paving behind it is a classic Chilterns arrangement, and a practical one: the toughest wear happens at the threshold.

Concrete blocks still have their place: modern homes, larger areas where budget matters, and the permeable formats discussed below. We steer colour choice conservatively — buffs, greys, brindles — so the driveway supports the house rather than competing with it.

Conservation areas and Article 4 streets

Standard block paving is an impermeable surface, so on a front garden over 5 m² it needs either drainage to a permeable area or a planning application. In conservation areas — and on streets with Article 4 directions, which exist in parts of Beaconsfield and the Chalfonts — the bar can be higher still, and material choice may be part of what the council considers. We check your street's designations on the design visit and design the drainage (or the application) accordingly, before you've committed to anything. Our guide to driveways in conservation areas and the Chilterns covers the local designations town by town.

Permeable block paving

Permeable blocks are a distinct product, not a laying technique: wider, chamfered joints filled with angular grit over an open-graded sub-base, so rain drains between the blocks. Done properly it passes the permeability test at any size — a clean way to keep a fully paved frontage within permitted development.

Patterns and edging that hold the design together

Herringbone

45° or 90° — the strongest interlock and the default for any surface that carries cars. 45° herringbone reads slightly softer against period elevations; 90° suits straighter, modern plots.

Stretcher bond & basketweave

Stretcher bond keeps long, narrow drives calm and makes small frontages feel larger. Basketweave is the traditional cottage choice — best on paths and lightly trafficked areas rather than the main running surface.

Borders, aprons & soldier courses

A contrasting soldier course contains the field visually and structurally; sett borders and entrance aprons add the hand-laid character period frontages ask for. Edging is set on concrete haunching so the perimeter never creeps.

Pattern isn't only aesthetics: interlock is structural. We'll tell you plainly where a pattern you like needs a stronger bond beneath the wheels — and where it doesn't.

Beneath the blocks: the specification that keeps it flat

Nearly every sunken driveway in South Bucks failed underground, not on the surface. Block paving is only as good as the layers you never see, so our quotations spell them out — and any quote you compare against ours should too:

  • Excavation to 200–250 mm below finished level for car traffic, deeper where ground is soft or made-up.
  • Geotextile membrane on clay ground — common toward the Chalfonts — so the sub-base can't pump down into the clay and the clay can't work up into the stone.
  • Sub-base of 150–200 mm compacted in two lifts, to falls of roughly 1:60–1:80 so water is heading the right way before a single block is laid.
  • Screeded laying course of sharp sand at 25–40 mm — consistent depth is what keeps the finished surface true.
  • Edge restraints haunched in concrete before the field is laid, and kiln-dried sand vibrated into the joints at completion.

Manholes, recesses and levels

Period frontages accumulate ironwork — inspection covers, stop-cocks, gullies. We re-set them in recessed trays so the pattern runs through visually, and adjust levels so covers sit flush rather than proud. It's detail work, and it's the difference you notice every time you walk to the car.

Widening onto the road? The kerb is a separate permission

If your project involves a new or widened vehicle access, the dropped kerb itself needs a crossover licence from Buckinghamshire Council's highways team and must be built by an approved contractor — separate from any planning question about the surface. We flag it during design so it never surprises you mid-project. The wider rules are in our planning permission guide.

Block paving questions, answered

Do I need planning permission for block paving?

If your front garden area is over 5 m² and you choose standard (impermeable) blocks, you need either drainage to a permeable area — a lawn, border or soakaway — or planning permission. Permeable block systems avoid the question entirely. On conservation-area and Article 4 streets we check the specific position for your address as part of the free design visit.

Clay or concrete — is clay really worth it?

For a period frontage, usually yes. Clay's colour is permanent, it weathers rather than fades, and it suits older elevations in a way pigmented concrete doesn't. On a modern property, or a large area where budget leads, a well-chosen concrete block in a muted tone is a perfectly good answer — and we'll say so.

Why do some block driveways sink in the first few years?

Almost always the sub-base: too shallow, poorly compacted, or laid on soft ground without correction. The blocks are just the visible layer. Our quotations state the dig-out depth and sub-base specification precisely so you can compare like with like — and our guarantee covers the build-up, not just the surface.

How do I maintain it?

Sweep, occasionally wash, top up the jointing sand every few years, and treat any weed growth early (it roots in the joints, not under the blocks). Sealing is optional: it deepens colour and slows weed growth but commits you to re-application. We cover both routes in the aftercare guidance at handover.

Where does the rainwater go?

On standard block paving we build falls toward planted areas, channel drains or a soakaway sized to your ground — never straight onto the highway, which is both bad practice and a planning problem. Permeable systems drain through the joints into an open-graded sub-base beneath.
