Resin driveway costs in 2026: the Buckinghamshire guide

Real per-m² ranges, three worked local examples, and the line items that move a quote — so you can read any resin quotation, including ours, like someone who knows what's underneath.

Guide · Updated July 2026 · Installed prices, South Buckinghamshire

How much does a resin driveway cost in 2026?

In South Buckinghamshire in 2026, expect £70–£90 per m² for a resin bound overlay on a sound existing base, and £110–£150 per m² for a full build-up with excavation and a new free-draining sub-base. A typical 60 m² frontage lands between £7,000 and £9,500 fully built.

National guides quote £60–£145/m², and the spread isn't dishonest — it's the overlay/full-build split doing the work. The single most important question in any resin quote is what's happening below the surface, which is why our worked examples below itemise it. (For what the layers actually are, see the build-up section of our resin driveways page.)

What moves the price up or down?

Five things, in rough order of impact: whether the existing base can be overlaid or must be dug out; total area (bigger is cheaper per m²); drainage needs (clay ground may need a soakaway, thresholds need channels); edging (existing walls are free, new sett borders aren't); and aggregate choice (UV-stable resin should be standard, not an extra).

  • Base condition — the difference between £70–£90 and £110–£150 per m². An honest assessment here is worth more than any discount.
  • Area — mobilisation, waste and machine time spread across more metres: 100 m² costs meaningfully less per m² than 30 m².
  • Drainage — £300–£900 as itemised lines where the ground needs it; nothing on free-draining chalk. Our SuDS guide explains when and why.
  • Edging — resin needs a firm shoulder: existing walls and kerbs cost nothing; new block or sett borders run £40–£70 per linear metre and lift the design.
  • Access and complexity — tight town-centre access, hand-digging around mature roots, deep made-up ground: all real, all visible on a site visit, none of them surprises if the quote was done properly.

How does resin compare with block paving and gravel on price?

On a 60 m² frontage: resin bound £7,000–£9,500, block paving £5,500–£8,000 (clay at the top of that band), stabilised gravel £3,500–£5,000. The groundworks beneath all three cost similar money — the surface choice is what separates the totals.

Price is one column of a bigger decision — lifespan, maintenance, planning position and looks are compared properly in the resin vs block paving vs gravel guide. And remember the planning asymmetry from our planning guide: resin bound and gravel are permeable and permission-free at any size; standard block paving over 5 m² needs its drainage designed.

How do you compare quotes fairly?

Line by line, not total by total. A meaningful resin quote states: excavation depth, sub-base stone type and depth, whether the resin is UV-stable, surface depth and aggregate size, edge detailing, drainage provision, and the guarantee's length and coverage. A cheaper total missing those lines isn't cheaper — it's incomplete.

The two lines most often missing from cheap quotes are the sub-base specification (standard Type 1 under a "permeable" surface doesn't drain — the commonest hidden defect in the trade) and drainage. Ask for both in writing. Ours arrive that way by default: request a quote and see.

Quick answers

Why do resin quotes vary so much for the same driveway?

Because they're rarely quoting the same job: one assumes an overlay, another a full dig-out; one prices UV-stable resin, another doesn't; one includes drainage, another leaves it to be "discovered" mid-job. Comparing line items instead of totals exposes the difference in minutes.

Is a cheap overlay ever the right call?

Yes — on a genuinely sound, crack-free, draining base, an overlay at £70–£90/m² is honest value; we quote plenty. It's wrong when the base is failing, because resin faithfully reproduces every crack beneath it within a couple of winters. The base test comes first, every time.

Does the aggregate colour change the price?

Between standard natural blends, barely. Speciality decorative blends carry a small premium, but the meaningful money is below the surface. Choose the blend that suits the house — the golden and flint tones we recommend for period frontages sit in the standard band.

When is the cheapest time of year to book?

Late autumn to late winter is quieter across the trade and scheduling is more flexible, though resin needs dry, frost-free laying windows so winter programmes carry weather-slip risk. Spring and summer book out earliest. The price difference is modest; the waiting-list difference isn't.

Are these prices fixed once quoted?

Ours are: the design visit exists so the quotation can be a fixed, itemised figure rather than an estimate with an appetite. The only changes come from genuine unknowns we've flagged in advance (buried tanks, unrecorded services), and those get agreed before work continues, not after.
