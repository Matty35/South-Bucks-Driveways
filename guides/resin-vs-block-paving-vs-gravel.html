Resin vs block paving vs gravel: the full comparison

Three surfaces dominate South Bucks driveways: resin bound, block paving and gravel. This guide compares them on 2026 cost, lifespan, maintenance, planning and appearance. One decision table first, then the detail.

Guide · Last updated: July 2026 · Prices are indicative installed ranges for our area

Which driveway surface is best?

There's no universal winner — there's a best fit per frontage. Resin bound wins for a seamless, low-maintenance, planning-free surface on family driveways. Block paving wins for period character and repairability. Gravel wins on cost for large frontages and on conservation-area sympathy. The table below decides most cases.

How do the three compare at a glance?

Gravel is cheapest (£45–£80/m²), resin bound and block paving overlap in the £75–£150/m² band. All three last 20+ years installed properly. Gravel and resin bound are permeable, so they never need planning permission; standard block paving does over 5 m² unless drainage is designed in.

What do they really cost in 2026?

On a typical 60 m² South Bucks frontage, expect roughly £7,000–£9,500 for resin bound (full build-up), £5,500–£8,000 for block paving depending on block choice, and £3,500–£5,000 for stabilised gravel. Groundworks are the leveller: excavation, sub-base and drainage cost much the same whatever goes on top.

That last point is the one homeowners are most often surprised by: roughly half of a full-build quote is below the surface, and it's the half that shouldn't be shaved. Per-material line items, worked examples and what moves prices up and down are in the 2026 resin cost guide — the groundwork logic applies to all three surfaces.

Which lasts longest and which is easiest to live with?

Block paving lasts longest — clay pavers can serve 40 years and any settled area can be lifted and re-laid invisibly. Resin bound is the easiest to live with day-to-day: nothing to rake, no joints to weed, a sweep and an occasional wash. Gravel asks for a little attention often, rather than a lot rarely.

Lifespan numbers assume the invisible parts were built right: a 150–200 mm compacted sub-base under blocks, an open-graded draining base under resin, membrane and grids under gravel. Skimp there and all three fail on the same schedule — the surface takes the blame, but the base did the failing. (Sinking blocks, fraying resin edges and balding gravel wheel-lines are all repairable: see driveway repairs.)

Which is best for a period home or conservation area?

Gravel first, then natural-toned resin bound, then muted clay paving — that's the order of least friction for conservation frontages. All three can be right; what matters is tone and restraint. Bright paviours, fake cobbles and stamped concrete are the choices Chilterns design guidance warns against.

The reasoning, street by street, is in our conservation areas guide — including which local towns carry designations and what Article 4 directions mean for frontage works. The short version: choose the surface your street would have had, in the colour the ground around here actually is.

Which is best for a large frontage?

Stabilised gravel, usually — at 200 m²-plus the per-m² saving compounds into five figures, and the country-house look suits big South Bucks plots. The smart hybrid is common: resin bound or setts for the apron and parking court where cars turn hardest, gravel for the approach.

Hybrids also solve the physics: turning zones get the bound surface that shrugs off power steering, slopes get resin (gravel migrates downhill), and the long run gets the economical permeable field. Zoning like this is standard on our larger designs — and it's the configuration in the "country plot near Penn" worked example in the cost guide.

What about planning permission?

Resin bound and gravel are permeable, so neither ever needs surfacing permission. Standard block paving over 5 m² of front garden needs rainwater directed to a permeable area — a designed detail, not a deal-breaker — or a permeable-block system. Article 4 and conservation-area streets need a designation check whatever the surface.

The full rules, including which surfaces count as permeable and what happens if you ignore it, are in the planning permission guide; the drainage engineering that makes impermeable surfaces compliant is in the SuDS drainage guide.

Quick answers

Is resin bound worth the premium over gravel?

On a family driveway used hard every day, usually yes: no raking, no migration, no stone in the hallway, and a seamless finish gravel can't give. On a long approach or a lightly used second entrance, gravel's economics are hard to argue with — which is why hybrids are often the honest recommendation.

Does block paving add the most value to a house?

Agents locally rate a well-kept driveway highly whatever the surface; what hurts value is a failing one. Clay block on a period frontage and clean resin on a modern one both present well. Spend on the build-up and the design, not on the theory of resale.

Can I mix surfaces without it looking bitty?

Yes — the trick is hierarchy: one field surface, one accent. A sett apron with a resin field, or a gravel approach with a block parking court, reads as designed. Three surfaces in one view rarely does.

Which surface handles a sloped drive best?

Resin bound or block paving — both are bound surfaces that hold a gradient. Loose gravel migrates downhill no matter the grid once a slope steepens beyond a gentle fall; on gravel schemes we switch the sloped section to resin bound gravel so the appearance carries through.

What would you put on your own driveway?

Depends which of our houses you ask about: resin bound on the modern family frontage, gridded gravel with a sett apron on the long village plot. That's the honest answer to most comparison questions — the frontage picks the surface.
